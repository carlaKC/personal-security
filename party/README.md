# Running a Security Party

This guide is for the facilitator of a security party. It covers what the party is for, the risks of running one, how to run it and which documents to share with participants.

You don't need to be a security expert, but you should have a reasonable understanding of security best practices, and be able to help someone who gets stuck.

## What Success Looks Like

The party is a success if everyone:
- Understands what to look out for now, which they didn't need to before.
- Has taken a few concrete steps: at minimum, setting up a password manager with two-factor login and adding a couple of passwords to it.

It's a bonus if people feel confident enough to run one with their own friends, or to help their parents get set up.

## Risks

A security party means handling sensitive information in a room full of other people, which is a real risk:
- Participants: other people may see their passwords, recovery codes or which accounts they use.
- You: if someone later gets locked out or has an account compromised, you're the person who helped. You want to be able to say with confidence that you never saw or touched anyone's secrets.

The bet this party makes is that a small risk among trusted friends is worth it to fix much bigger risks from everyone else. That only holds if the group trusts each other and everyone follows the rules of engagement, so don't run a party with strangers.

## Rules of Engagement

Participants get these rules in [Before the Party](participants/01-prepare.md), and you'll go through them in the opening talk:
1. Don't look at anyone else's screen, and keep yours to yourself while you type passwords or codes.
2. Don't touch anyone else's device. Help by pointing and explaining.
3. Never say passwords or codes out loud, or write them down for someone else.
4. Keep recovery codes face down, and take them home.
5. No photos or screen recordings.
6. Lock your phone when you put it down.
7. Anyone can skip a step and do it at home instead, no questions asked.

These apply to you too. It's tempting to grab someone's phone to "just quickly fix it", but don't.

## Shape of the Party

1. Opening talk (~15 min): explain what's changed, what matters, the rules of engagement and what we're doing today.
2. Checklist (~90 min): everyone works through the party checklist, while you (and any other technical friends) help.
3. Wrap up (~10 min): fill in follow-ups, share the take-home page and mention the parents guide.

These timings are guesses, and will be updated after the first run.
