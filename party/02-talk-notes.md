# Talk Notes

These are talking points for the facilitator's opening talk, which should take about 15 minutes. The goal is for everyone to understand why they're doing the checklist, so that the rest of the evening can be spent doing it. Use your own words and examples.

## What's Changed

- Scams used to be expensive to run against individuals, so most people got away with weak security.
- AI makes convincing, personalized scams cheap to produce at scale.
- Phishing used to be easy to spot because of bad spelling and grammar. AI-written messages are fluent, and can include real details about you.
- Voices can be cloned from a short clip, and video calls can be faked.

## What Still Works

- Scams still rely on the same tactics: urgency, secrecy and unusual payment methods (gift cards, crypto, wire transfers, "safe accounts"). These are the signals to watch for, not whether something looks or sounds real.
- Verify through a channel you already trust: hang up and call back on a saved number.

## What Matters Most

- Email: almost every account can be reset from your email, so it's the most important account to protect.
- Phone: it receives your login codes and holds your accounts.
- Password reuse: one breach unlocks every account that shares the password. A password manager remembers unique passwords for you.
- Two-factor login: a stolen password isn't enough to get into your account.

## Today's Plan

- Walk through the sections of the checklist.
- The goal is a good starting state: a password manager with two-factor login, and a couple of passwords in it. Moving the rest of your passwords over is homework.

## Risks and Rules of Engagement

- Getting together to do this is itself a risk: we're handling passwords and recovery codes in a room full of people. That's why this is only for friends who trust each other.
- Go through the [rules of engagement](README.md#rules-of-engagement) one by one, and check that everyone agrees to them.
- If anyone thinks someone saw a password or code, they should change it after the party. No hard feelings.
- Anyone can skip a step and do it at home instead.

## Avoiding Lockouts

The most likely thing to go wrong today is someone locking themselves out of their own account.
- Check recovery details and save recovery codes before turning on two-factor login.
- Only change an important password if you can still receive codes on your phone.

## Parents

- Introduce the parents guide. Older relatives are common targets for these scams, and probably aren't in the room.
- IMO agreeing on a family safe word is the most valuable thing people can do after the party.

## Common Questions

- Is it risky to keep all my passwords in one place? It's a tradeoff, and a good one. One strong, well-protected vault is much safer than reused passwords.
- Should I use the built-in password manager? Apple Passwords or Google Password Manager work well if all your devices are from the same company. If you mix devices, a cross-platform manager (eg Bitwarden or 1Password) is easier.
- Are text message codes bad? They're better than nothing, but can be intercepted if someone takes over your phone number. Use an authenticator app or passkey where possible.
- What's a passkey? A login that your phone handles with Face ID or a fingerprint. There's no password to steal, so use one when it's offered.
- Why would anyone target me? Most attacks are automated and target everyone. AI makes that even cheaper.
- What about AI chatbots? Don't share passwords, ID numbers or other people's private information. Assume anything you type may be stored.
